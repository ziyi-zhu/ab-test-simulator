Relative lift, final read

The likelihood is what the data say on their own: a normal curve centred on the observed lift, with the delta-method standard error. The posterior is a precision-weighted blend of prior and likelihood. The shaded tail is the posterior probability that test is worse than control.

Prior sensitivity

Same data, every prior width

Holding the data fixed, this sweeps the prior's standard error from very tight to very loose. A tight prior drags the estimate to the prior mean. A loose prior lets the data speak, and the Bayesian answer converges to the frequentist one.

Daily reads

Cumulative reads of one run

Real dashboards update daily, and people look. Early intervals are wide and estimates swing. A plain p-value can dip under α by chance; the sequential interval stays wide until enough data has arrived.

Monte Carlo

2,000 runs · current settings

One run is an anecdote. Here the same design is repeated 2,000 times with no true effect (A/A) and 2,000 times with your true lift. A good procedure makes few calls in the A/A world and many correct calls in the A/B world. The black tick marks α.

Frequentist (Statsig default)Bayesian
Question answeredIf there were no effect, how surprising is this data?Given my prior and this data, how likely is test better, and how much do I lose if I'm wrong?
Headline outputRelative delta, 95% CI (±1.96·SE), p-value from a two-sided z-testPosterior mean, credible interval, chance to beat P(Δ>0), expected loss E[Δ | Δ<0]·P(Δ<0)
PriorNoneNormal prior on relative ATE. Flat N(0,∞) by default; informed priors usually come from past experiments' average effect and standard error
Looking earlyInflates false positives unless you use sequential testing (mSPRT widens intervals until the planned end)The posterior is always "valid" as a belief, but a ship rule applied daily still makes more false calls (see the Monte Carlo table)
With a flat priorThe two agree numerically: credible interval = confidence interval, and chance to beat = 1 − p/2 for a positive effect. Differences come from the prior and from the decision rule.
Main riskMisreading p as "probability the change works"; peeking; underpowered tests that exaggerate effectsA confident but wrong prior can ship a loser or bury a winner; results depend on a choice others must trust
